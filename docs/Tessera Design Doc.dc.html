03 — ENGINE

Two canvases and a DOM shell

You asked me to recommend and justify. The board is Canvas 2D; everything that is chrome — top bar, tray container, sheets, buttons, hint button — is DOM. Not WebGL, not DOM pieces.

Why not DOM/SVG pieces

250 elements each carrying a clip-path, a shadow, and a transform will composite acceptably on an M-series iPad and badly on an iPhone 12. Worse, the light thesis needs additive blending — assembled area emitting onto the mat — and CSS gives you no honest way to do that at 60fps. DOM pieces also make hit-testing a lie: the browser hit-tests the bounding box, so overlapping tabs pick the wrong piece, and you end up re-implementing point-in-path anyway.

Why not WebGL yet

At 250 pieces there is nothing WebGL buys you that a per-frame drawImage loop over pre-rendered piece bitmaps does not. It buys real cost: shader authoring, context-loss handling on iOS backgrounding, and text/UI becoming your problem. Build the renderer behind a thin interface (draw(scene, camera)) so a WebGL backend can slot in the day 1000-piece boards ship.

The layer stack

The split matters because the static layer holds the expensive thing — the assembled image and its bloom — and it only changes on a placement. The dynamic layer redraws every frame but is usually carrying fewer than twenty objects: the pieces the camera can see plus whatever is in your hand. On an idle board with no finger down, both layers are still and the app draws nothing at all. That is how the iPad stays cool for a sixty-minute session.

Non-negotiable performance rules

  • Cutting happens in a Web Worker with OffscreenCanvas. The main thread never blocks; the setup screen shows real progress.
  • Piece bitmaps are rendered once at min(devicePixelRatio, 2) and never re-rasterised while zooming. Zoom scales the bitmap; a re-raster pass runs 200ms after the pinch settles, only for on-screen pieces, only above 1.5× zoom.
  • Source images are downscaled to a max 2560px long edge before cutting. Nobody can see more, and it halves memory.
  • Hit-testing is a spatial hash over piece bounds, then point-in-path against the cached Path2D. Never a full scan.
  • Budget: 250 pieces, iPhone 12, sustained 60fps while dragging a 20-piece island. If that fails, the fix is fewer live objects, not a smaller image.
04 — THE CUT

How a photo becomes pieces

You asked specifically about this, and it is the part most worth pinning down before code, because everything else — snapping, resume, sync, cut styles — inherits its shape. The whole cut is deterministic from a seed, which is the single most useful property it can have: the same puzzle id always produces the same pieces on every device, so a saved game is a few hundred bytes rather than a few megabytes, and Duo mode later gets identical boards for free.

Step 1 — Choose the grid

Piece counts are targets. Given image aspect a = W/H and target N, start at cols = round(sqrt(N·a)), rows = round(N/cols), then search ±2 in both directions for the pair that minimises a weighted sum of count error and piece-aspect error, rejecting anything where piece aspect falls outside 0.82–1.22. A 3:2 photo at "200" lands on 17×12 = 204. Show the real number everywhere, never the target.

Step 2 — Jitter the lattice

Build a (cols+1) × (rows+1) vertex lattice. Every interior vertex gets a random offset of up to ±0.12 × piece size, drawn from a seeded PRNG (mulberry32 seeded on the puzzle id). Border vertices stay pinned so the outer frame is a clean rectangle. This one step is the difference between a puzzle that looks machine-stamped and one that looks cut — with a perfect lattice, every piece is interchangeable at a glance and the search stops being fun.

Step 3 — Draw the edges

Each interior edge between two vertices is generated once and shared by the two pieces that meet on it — one gets it as drawn, the other as its exact reverse. This is what guarantees pieces physically interlock rather than merely appearing to. The edge is a canonical cubic-bezier chain in normalised edge space (0,0)→(1,0) with a knob at t ≈ 0.5 ± 0.06: neck width 0.20, head radius 0.14, protrusion 0.22 × edge length with ±10% jitter, polarity flipped by a seeded coin toss. Transform that unit path onto the real edge vector and you have the piece outline. Boundary edges are straight lines.

Cut styles are one function. The entire style system is the signature edgePath(a, b, polarity, rng) → Path2D. Classic ships in MVP. Ribbon, Organic, and Geometric are later implementations of that same signature and touch nothing else in the codebase. Build the interface now even though only one implementation exists.

Step 4 — Rasterise

Each piece's bitmap is its cell bounds expanded by the maximum protrusion plus 2px of bleed. Draw the closed path, set source-in, draw the source image at the right offset. Then bake the material in the same pass: a 1px inner highlight stroked on the upper-left of the path at 8% white, a 1px inner shadow on the lower-right at 22% black. Baking it means the bevel is free at runtime and always reads correctly regardless of zoom. Store as ImageBitmap, transferred back from the worker.

Step 5 — Emit the graph

The output that actually matters is not the images, it is the adjacency graph: for every piece, its four neighbour ids (or null), and its exact target offset from each. Snapping never asks "am I near my correct absolute position on the board" as its first question — it asks "am I near where a graph-neighbour says I should be." That single choice is what makes free-floating islands work identically to placing on the board frame, with no special-case code.

Cost, measured

250 pieces from a 2560px image at 2× is roughly 250 × (190px)² × 4 bytes ≈ 36 MB of bitmaps — comfortable. Cut time in a worker: target under 1.2s on an iPhone 12, which is short enough that the setup screen's "cutting" state is a genuine beat of anticipation rather than a wait. Show pieces materialising onto the mat as they arrive from the worker; it turns loading into the first moment of delight.

05 — STATE

The board state machine

Three state machines, deliberately kept separate. Session lifecycle, pointer interaction, and piece membership. Conflating them is how jigsaw apps end up with pieces stuck to fingers after a phone call comes in.

Session machine

idle → sourcing → cropping → configuring → cutting → playing ⇄ paused
playing → completing → card → (newPuzzle | library)
any → interrupted (backgrounded, low memory) → restoring → playing

interrupted is a first-class state, not an error path. On visibilitychange the timer stops, any held piece is released to its current position, and a snapshot is written synchronously. Coming back always lands on the board, never on a modal apologising.

Pointer machine

{{ p.name }}
{{ p.note }}

Gesture arbitration, in strict priority: two fingers always means camera (pinch-zoom, pan, and — in Rotation mode — twist to rotate the held piece if one exists, otherwise nothing). One finger on a piece means drag. One finger on empty mat means pan. There is no tap-to-select-then-tap-to-place; direct manipulation only, because the entire product promise is weight in the hand.

Piece and cluster model

Piece { id, col, row, targetX, targetY, neighbours[4], bitmap, path,
        clusterId, x, y, rot }
Cluster { id, pieceIds[], x, y, rot, anchored:bool, kind: 'loose'|'island'|'board' }
Board   { clusters: Map, unionFind, placedCount, edgeFrameComplete }

Every piece belongs to exactly one cluster; a single loose piece is a cluster of one. Cluster 0 is the board itself — anchored, unrotatable, and the only cluster whose coordinates are absolute. Everything else floats. Merging is union-find: when a snap resolves, the smaller cluster is transformed into the larger's frame and absorbed. Merging with cluster 0 is what "placed" means, and the completion condition is simply cluster0.pieceIds.length === N.

This is why islands need almost no new code. An island is a cluster with kind:'island', a user-visible label, and a position on the mat margin. It drags, rotates, and snaps by the same rules as a single piece. The only special behaviour is presentational: islands render with a faint containing outline and a mono label chip, and they can be collapsed to that chip to reclaim mat space.

Snap resolution, in order

  1. On release, gather every piece in the dragged cluster that has a graph-neighbour outside it.
  2. For each such pair, compute positional error between where the neighbour is and where it should be relative to the dragged piece, plus rotational error.
  3. Discard candidates over tolerance: 0.28 × piece size for Standard (0.18 Precise, 0.40 Generous), and 12° of rotation in Rotation mode.
  4. Of the survivors, take the lowest combined error. Ties break toward the cluster containing more pieces, then toward cluster 0 — so an ambiguous drop near the board prefers the board.
  5. No survivor: the cluster stays exactly where it was dropped. Dull tap, no penalty, no bounce-back. Bounce-back is the single most infuriating pattern in this category — it discards the player's spatial intent.
06 — TRAY

Filters are lenses, never sorts

You said the thing that worries you is players not sticking with it, and named the persistent tray layout as part of the answer. You are right, and it has a precise design consequence: the tray has one canonical order that never changes unless the player changes it. Filters hide and reveal within that order; they do not reflow it. Turn a filter off and every remaining piece is exactly where you left it. Muscle memory — "the dark green one is two rows down on the left" — is the main reason a 250-piece session survives a coffee break, and every competitor destroys it by re-sorting on each filter change.

Lenses

{{ f.name }}
{{ f.note }}

Colour clustering, done honestly

Six bins, k-means in OKLab over each piece's mean colour, weighted so lightness contributes less than hue — otherwise a photo of a forest gives you six shades of "dark" and the filter is useless. Bins are labelled by their centroid rendered as a swatch plus a numeral, because colour must never be the only signal. Pieces whose internal colour variance is high (a piece straddling sky and roof) go into a seventh "mixed" bin rather than being forced into a lie.

Shelf and islands

A pinned shelf row sits at the top of the tray and survives every lens: drag a piece there to say "I am working on this one." Long-press enters multi-select; selected pieces show a numbered order badge; Pull out lifts them onto the mat as a labelled island, auto-arranged in a loose grid so they are immediately workable. This is the sub-assembly behaviour from the brief, and it is the feature that makes a 250-piece board feel like a table rather than a scroll list.

Form factors

iPad landscape: docked right, 300–380pt, resizable by dragging its inner edge, always visible. iPhone portrait: a bottom sheet at three detents — peek (one row of pieces, ~96pt), half, and full — with the lens chips pinned to the sheet header so they are reachable one-handed. Dragging a piece out of the sheet auto-collapses it to peek and re-expands on release, so the mat is never obscured mid-drag.

07 — LIGHT

One light system, four jobs

Progress bloom, hint glow, group-merge seam, and completion payoff are all the same renderer feature at different intensities. Implement it once: a downsampled copy of the static layer, blurred, drawn back with globalCompositeOperation:'lighter', with a per-region intensity mask. Everything below is a value fed into that mask.

{{ g.name }}
{{ g.note }}

Hint tiers

Tier 1 (Warm, free, unlimited, the default button behaviour): tap a loose piece, then the hint button — a 3×3-piece region of the board breathes for 3 seconds. Two full breaths, 1400ms each, peak intensity 0.55, edges feathered over a piece-and-a-half so no exact slot is legible. It answers "where do I look," which is the honest help.

Tier 2 (Guide, costs 1): the exact slot outlines at 1px in accent, and the held or selected piece translates 6px toward it and holds, as if magnetised, with a faint light trail. If the piece is in the tray, its chip leans instead. Tier 3 (Place, costs 2): auto-place with the full snap treatment — same spring, same audio, same ripple. Never a diminished version; a player who needed help still deserves the good part.

In Zen every tier is free and the counter is simply absent — not greyed out, not showing "∞". The absence is the message.

Audio, haptics, and what the web loses

The pitch ladder walks a pentatonic scale up seven steps and holds at the top; it breaks on a wrong drop or eight idle seconds and resets to root. Pitch-shift only the transient layer via playback rate — the body and the 200ms tail stay at 1.0, or the room appears to shrink as you get faster. Four round-robin samples per layer with ±3% detune. Ladder off entirely in Zen.

Unlock the AudioContext on the first deliberate tap of the first run screen, pre-decode every buffer during the cutting step, and duck the ambient bed 4dB for 300ms under each snap. A snap that arrives 80ms late is worse than no snap; latency is a design property here, not an engineering detail.

09 — MOTION

The two motions that matter

The snap — frame by frame

Time
What happens
{{ s.t }}
{{ s.what }}

Spring, not easing: stiffness 520, damping 26, mass 1, integrated per frame from the release velocity — so a piece flicked at the slot arrives hot and overshoots ~2px, while a piece gently placed just settles. That velocity inheritance is most of the perceived physicality, and it is exactly what a fixed cubic-bezier cannot give you. Reduced motion: skip the overshoot and the ripple, keep the light bloom and the audio, and place in 120ms linear.

Hint glow — frame by frame

{{ s.t }}
{{ s.what }}

Group merge: both cluster outlines flash once at 40% for 120ms, then the shared seam light-bleeds outward over 260ms and the chord plays, pitched by combined cluster size. Edge-frame completion gets its own beat — the entire border traces once in accent light over 600ms, clockwise from the top-left corner.

Token sheet

Ship exactly this as :root custom properties. Mat finishes override only the four surface tokens; the accent group is written at runtime by the extractor. Note the extractor clamp — it is the difference between a bloom and a stain.

Extractor clamp: take the three dominant colours in OKLab, then force L into 0.62–0.78 and C into 0.09–0.16 before use. 14 — PERSISTENCE

Local first, account only when earned

Everything works signed out, forever. Supabase enters only when the player touches something that inherently needs identity — a streak they want to keep across devices, or a leaderboard. The prompt at that moment says what it buys, not what it demands.

Save format (full-fidelity resume)

SessionSnapshot {
  puzzleId, seed, cols, rows, mode, assists, version,
  pieces: Float32Array [x, y, rot, clusterId] × N,  // packed, base64
  clusters: [{ id, kind, label, x, y, rot, collapsed }],
  camera: { x, y, zoom },
  tray: { order[], pinned[], lens, lensArg, scroll },
  timer: { elapsedMs, running:false }, hintsUsed, cleanRun
}

Roughly 6 KB for a 250-piece board — small enough to write on every placement without thinking about it. Because the cut is seeded, no geometry and no images are stored; the snapshot restores a board bit-for-bit on any device. Write to IndexedDB debounced at 800ms, plus a synchronous write on visibilitychange. Source photos live in IndexedDB as blobs, and only upload to Supabase Storage if the player signs in and opts into sync.

15 — RETENTION

Your actual worry: why they stick with it

You said the risk is players tiring of the mechanic. That is the right thing to be afraid of, because the mechanic genuinely does not change — you place pieces. Jigsaw fatigue almost never comes from boredom with placing; it comes from three specific failures, and each has a design answer.

{{ r.fail }}
{{ r.fix }}

The starter library — 50 photos, curated not dumped

Your fifty open-source landscape, geography, and animal photos are a design asset, not a content dump. Three rules. First, curate for cuttability: reject any photo with more than ~25% near-uniform area (open sky, flat snow) at counts above 150, or tag it "hard" and let it be a badge of honour rather than a bad surprise. Second, browse by feeling, not folder: shelves like "wide and calm", "dense and busy", "one animal, close" beat "Landscape / Geography / Animals", because the player is choosing a mood for the next forty minutes. Third, pre-compute everything — dominant colours, difficulty rating, recommended piece counts — at build time, so a curated photo opens instantly while an uploaded one takes a second.

Each entry needs licence and attribution stored alongside it, surfaced quietly on the completion card. Source from Unsplash, Pexels, and public-domain museum collections; verify per-image licence rather than trusting the platform default.

One more lever worth building early because it is nearly free: "puzzle this again, harder." A photo they loved at 100 pieces is a genuinely different puzzle at 250, and re-cutting an existing photo skips every friction point in the funnel — no picking, no cropping, no configuring. It is the cheapest repeat session in the product.

16 — FIRST RUN

The guided twelve

You chose a taught first puzzle over pure discovery. Done wrong that is a tutorial wall; done right it is the best sixty seconds in the product, because a 12-piece board with a beautiful photo is already the whole game and finishing it feels good rather than instructional.

  1. Open on a curated 12-piece photo already scattered, no copy but "Drag a piece where you think it goes." No account, no menu, no mode picker.
  2. First correct snap: full juice. Nothing is explained; the sound and the light explain it.
  3. At four placed, the tray slides in on its own carrying the remaining pieces, with one line: "Pieces live here. Filter them." The lens chips pulse once.
  4. At eight placed, if 20 seconds pass with no placement, the hint button glows and fires tier 1 unprompted, once. The player learns the hint exists by being rescued by it, not by reading about it.
  5. On completion: the card, then "Now use your own photo" as primary and "Today's puzzle" as secondary.

Skippable from a small "skip" at all times, never modal, and it counts as a real completion on the collection wall. A tutorial the player is allowed to be proud of is not a tutorial.
